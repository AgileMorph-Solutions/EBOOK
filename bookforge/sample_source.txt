FORGOTTEN BRITISH MEALS — SOURCE NOTES

Beef Dripping on Toast
In working-class British homes through the 1930s and 1940s, beef dripping was the
fat skimmed and saved from a Sunday roast. It was spread thickly on toasted bread
and seasoned with salt and pepper. The dark, savoury jelly that settled at the
bottom of the dripping pot was prized most of all and was often spread on bread as
a treat. Dripping was valued because nothing from the joint of meat was wasted, an
attitude reinforced by wartime rationing, which began in Britain in 1940. A jar of
dripping could keep for weeks in a cool larder.

Bread and Dripping as a Meal
For many families, bread and dripping served as a cheap supper or an after-school
snack for children. It delivered calories and flavour at almost no cost. The meal
largely disappeared after the 1950s as vegetable fats and margarine became cheaper
and as concerns about animal fat grew in later decades.

Pease Pudding
Pease pudding is a thick, savoury purée made from split yellow peas, simmered for
hours, often with a ham hock or bacon for flavour. It was especially common in the
North East of England, where it was sold alongside boiled ham and eaten in a bread
roll, a combination still found in parts of the region. The dish dates back
centuries and was a staple source of protein for poorer households before meat was
affordable on a daily basis.

Brawn (Head Cheese)
Brawn was made by slowly boiling a pig's head until the meat fell from the bone,
then setting the chopped meat in its own savoury jelly. Once cold and sliced, it
was eaten with bread, pickles, or vinegar. Brawn reflected the nose-to-tail tradition
of home butchery, in which every part of an animal was used. It was common on cold
tables and at funerals and gatherings, but fell out of favour as packaged sliced
meats spread through supermarkets.

Tripe and Onions
Tripe, the lining of a cow's stomach, was simmered slowly with onions in milk to
make a mild, creamy dish. It was a popular cheap supper in industrial towns of the
North of England, and dedicated tripe shops were once a common sight in places like
Lancashire. Tripe consumption declined sharply in the second half of the twentieth
century as tastes changed and cheaper cuts of conventional meat became widely
available.
