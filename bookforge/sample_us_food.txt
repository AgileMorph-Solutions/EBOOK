HOW AMERICA ATE — SOURCE NOTES
Forgotten foods, inventions, and habits that shaped the American table.

The TV Dinner
The frozen "TV Dinner" was introduced by the Swanson company in 1953. The meals
came in a segmented aluminum tray that could go straight into the oven, and the
first version was built around turkey with cornbread dressing, peas, and sweet
potatoes. A popular account holds that Swanson created them to use up a large
surplus of Thanksgiving turkey. The product was marketed to fit the new American
habit of eating in front of the television, which was spreading rapidly into homes
during the 1950s.

Breakfast Cereal and Battle Creek
Ready-to-eat breakfast cereal grew out of health reform in Battle Creek, Michigan.
Dr. John Harvey Kellogg ran a sanitarium there and promoted plain, grain-based
foods. Corn flakes were developed at the sanitarium in the 1890s. In 1906 his
brother, Will Keith Kellogg, founded the company that became Kellogg's and added
sugar to make the flakes more appealing to a mass market. Battle Creek attracted
many imitators and became known as the cereal capital of the United States.

Frozen Food and Clarence Birdseye
Clarence Birdseye pioneered the flash-freezing of food, inspired by his observation
of Arctic fishing, where fish froze almost instantly in the extreme cold and tasted
fresh when later thawed. His quick-freezing process preserved food far better than
slow freezing. Birdseye's packaged frozen foods first went on retail sale in 1930
in Springfield, Massachusetts. Flash freezing laid the groundwork for the modern
frozen-food aisle.

The Supermarket
King Kullen, opened by Michael Cullen in 1930 in the Queens borough of New York
City, is often cited as the first true American supermarket. It combined self-
service, low prices, large volume, and ample parking under one roof. The format
spread quickly during the Great Depression because it let families buy food more
cheaply than at small clerk-served grocery stores.

Victory Gardens
During both World War I and World War II, the United States government encouraged
citizens to plant "victory gardens" to grow their own vegetables and ease pressure
on the food supply. During World War II these home and community gardens produced a
large share of the fresh vegetables consumed in the country. The campaign tied home
cooking and gardening directly to the war effort.

Spam
Spam, a canned pork product, was introduced by the Hormel company in 1937. Its long
shelf life made it valuable during World War II, when large quantities were shipped
to feed soldiers. The product became a lasting, if sometimes mocked, fixture of the
American pantry and remains especially popular in Hawaii.

Standardized Measuring and the Cookbook
Fannie Farmer helped standardize American home cooking by insisting on level, exact
measurements rather than vague handfuls and pinches. Her "Boston Cooking-School Cook
Book," published in 1896, popularized precise measurement and helped make recipes
reliably repeatable in ordinary kitchens.
